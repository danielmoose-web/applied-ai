Learning to use AI with judgment.

I created Applied AI Lab to teach people how AI systems work, how to check their outputs, and when human review is necessary. Foundations introduces these ideas; Studio asks learners to apply them to workplace problems.

  1. Start with verification.Open Foundations and choose “Trust, but Verify.” Consider what evidence you would need before relying on an AI-generated claim.
  2. Examine human responsibility.In “The Human in the Loop,” consider who reviews the output, who approves its use, and when the work should be escalated.
  3. Apply the decision to a workflow.In “Build a Better Workflow,” connect those checks to a repetitive task. Identify what AI can assist with and where a person remains accountable.
How Studio extends the practice

Studio organizes eight workplace scenarios around five stages: Discover, Design, Build, Break, and Explain. Learners investigate a need, propose a workflow, examine failures, and explain their choices to a specific audience.

Explorer provides guided practice. Builder reduces the scaffolding. Architect asks learners to make and defend more of the decisions independently. Every game is designed to be independently accessible.

The public Studio demo offers browser-local practice and downloadable progress backups. The private pilot is separate from this public portfolio.

What I would measure in a workplace pilot

I would establish a baseline, then evaluate work quality, time required, and whether participants can apply the checks independently. Completion alone would not establish proficiency.

Before collecting learner results, I would define consent, access, retention, and how the findings would guide follow-up training. Sample dashboards would be identified as illustrative.

How I lead the work

I created and own Applied AI Lab. I set its purpose, scope, and learning standards, then directed the work from course design through development, testing, and publication. I used AI tools to assist development and reviewed the resulting content and behavior.

My work has included deciding what to build first, establishing criteria for acceptable performance, and revising the product after learner testing. My requirements include independently accessible scenarios, optional reporting, and recovery of saved work; these remain criteria to verify in each deployment. I also set an editorial standard: explain the ideas clearly without removing the technical distinctions learners need to understand.

I approach AI education as a program that needs direction, evidence, and continued review. A course must teach the right material, give people useful practice, and provide a way to judge what they can do independently. Those decisions draw on my experience teaching, leading programs, and evaluating AI work.
